# Story flow

## Setting
Meranti is a fictional medium-size workplace. It has ordinary supplier invoices, shared documents, staff records, managers and an IT helpdesk. The course does not invent a government mandate, sector regulation or real customer procedure.

## People
| Character | Responsibility | Teaching use |
|---|---|---|
| Aina | Operations | Documents, public profile, account approvals and reporting |
| Farid | Finance | Bank changes, verification and payment authorisation |
| Mei | IT support | Genuine helpdesk process versus impersonation |
| Ravi | Manager | Authority pressure and support for verification |
| Nadia | Fictional supplier contact | Supplier-account/request verification |

Use consistent initials and labels. Avatars are fictional identity aids. No photorealistic depiction of an actual customer staff member is needed.

## Narrative architecture
M0 opens with Farid's payment-change baseline and reveals its fictional outcome. M1–M5 revisit the same workplace through separate side scenes, each introducing one decision family. Aina's profile scene is not asserted to have caused the original invoice incident. M6 introduces a new composite exercise where related evidence may suggest a connection, but participants must distinguish that inference from verified facts. M7 turns decisions into a reporting/recovery routine.

## Knowledge boundaries
Show only what the employee can know at that moment. The supplier denial and Ravi's denial become known on SL05. Do not colour the original email to expose its answer. Aina reporting an approval is a fact; account compromise is a hypothesis for IT to investigate. The tabletop should reward stating uncertainty, not inventing a full attack chain.

## Repeated scene rhythm
Present evidence, ask a decision, hear reasons, reveal additional context, practise the safer action, connect to primary evidence. Alternate brief explanation with inspection or discussion. No autoplay and no repetitive quiz on every screen.

## Emotional tone
Busy employees trying to do their jobs. Avoid caricatures, fear-driven threats and public blame. Use language such as 'Thank you for reporting. What did you see, and what did you do?' rather than 'Why did you click?' Give managers a role in protecting time for verification.

## Outcome paths
A safer choice can hold a payment or interrupt access. An unsafe choice leads to a supportive recovery discussion. Branches converge after feedback so the course fits its timetable. Include real legitimate requests; process compliance can still be required after identity verification.
